## (a) 

> $f(n) = e^{2n}, g(n) = e^n$: Prove or disprove $f(n) = O(g(n))$

**Big-O Definition**:  
$f(n) = O(g(n))$ means there exists a constant $c > 0$ and $n_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \geq n_0$:  
$$ f(n) \leq c \cdot g(n). $$

Substitute $f(n) = e^{2n}$ and $g(n) = e^n$:  
$$ e^{2n} \leq c \cdot e^n. $$  
Divide both sides by $e^n$:  
$$ e^n \leq c. $$  

As $n \to \infty$, $e^n$ grows unbounded, so no constant $c$ can satisfy this inequality for all $n \geq n_0$. Thus, **$f(n) \neq O(g(n))$**.

## (b) 

> $f(n) = n!, g(n) = n^n$: Prove or disprove $f(n) = o(g(n))$

**Small-o Definition**:  
$f(n) = o(g(n))$ means for every $c > 0$, there exists $n_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \geq n_0$:  
$$ f(n) \leq c \cdot g(n). $$

Substitute $f(n) = n!$ and $g(n) = n^n$:  
$$ n! \leq c \cdot n^n. $$  

Using Stirling's approximation for $n!$:  
$$ n! \sim \sqrt{2 \pi n} \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n. $$  
Divide both sides by $n^n$:  
$$ \frac{n!}{n^n} \sim \frac{\sqrt{2 \pi n}}{e^n}. $$  

As $n \to \infty$, the exponential term $e^n$ in the denominator dominates, and $\frac{n!}{n^n} \to 0$. This shows that $f(n) = o(g(n))$.  

### Final Answers:
(a) $f(n) \neq O(g(n))$ (Disproven).  
(b) $f(n) = o(g(n))$ (Proven).
