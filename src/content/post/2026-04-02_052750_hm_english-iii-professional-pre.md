## Week 2: Speaking Basics & Sentence Stress

### Stress

When speaking English, certain words are naturally stressed to convey meaning, while others are reduced.

> **Example:** "The `sun` `rises` in the `east`."
> *You must put heavier vocal stress on "sun", "rises", and "east".*

To know what to stress, words are divided into two categories:

- 1. Content Words (Stressed)
These carry the primary meaning of the sentence.
    - Nouns (e.g., sun, east, computer, presentation)
    - Adjectives (e.g., big, beautiful, important)
    - Verbs (e.g., rises, speak, analyze)
    - Adverbs (e.g., quickly, very, loudly)
    - Interrogatives (e.g., Who, when, why)
- 2. Function Words (Unstressed)
These are the grammatical "glue" of the sentence.
    - Prepositions (e.g., in, at, on)
    - Pronouns (e.g., he, she, it)
    - Determiners/Articles (e.g., the, a, an)
    - Conjunctions (e.g., and, but, or)
    - Auxiliary Verbs (e.g., is, are, have, do)
    - Particles (e.g., up, down, out)

### Intonation

Intonation is the rise and fall of your voice when speaking. Think of it as an arc curve that guides your listener through your sentence. Managing this curve is highly important when delivering a list.

Example: 
> "Come and enjoy our outdoor activities ↗, free food ↗, and a show with us ↘."

`Be` helps rise
interrogative(questions) words decrease.

Just to recap the rules we are adding:

* **Lists (Arc Curve):** Up on the first items ↗, down on the final item ↘.
* **Yes/No Questions:** Questions starting with a "Be" verb (Are, Is) or auxiliary verb usually have a **rising** intonation at the end ↗.
* **Wh- Questions:** Questions starting with interrogative words (Who, What, Where, Why, How) usually have a **falling** intonation at the end ↘.
* **Statements/Sentences:** Standard declarative sentences end with a **falling** intonation ↘. We also often use a slight rise ↗ at commas to show the thought isn't finished yet.

### Chunking
Breaking Down Long Sentences

When delivering a speech, reading word-by-word sounds robotic, but trying to say a long sentence all at once will leave you out of breath. **Chunking** is the practice of dividing sentences into smaller, manageable "thought groups" or phrases.

**Rules for Chunking:**

* **Group by Phrase:** 
    * Break sentences into logical grammatical phrases (like prepositional phrases or clauses).
* **Keep Connected Words Together:** 
    * Words that belong to the same thought or phrase cannot be separated. For example, never pause between an adjective and the noun it describes (e.g., keep "unusual weather" together).
* **Protect Important Words:** 
    * Ensure the core meaning remains intact within each chunk so the important words stand out.
* **Tame Long Sentences:** 
    * Use chunking whenever a sentence is simply too long to say comfortably in one single breath. Pause slightly between each chunk.

#### Example: The Weather Forecast


> Now for --- today's weather.

## Week 4: Delivering Your Message

### 1. Pause @ Commas
Punctuation completely changes the meaning of a sentence. A comma represents a hard pause. 

**Partner Exercise:** Take turns reading the following pairs. Make sure to clearly pause at the commas so your partner can guess which version you are saying.

* (1) He doesn't know Steve.  (2) He doesn't know, Steve.

* (1) If you're overweight, don't eat fast.  (2) If you're overweight, don't eat, fast!
* (1) Can you see Sue? (2) Can you see, Sue?
* (1) Dave Johnson isn't here. (2) Dave, Johnson isn't here.
* (1) It is there for the third time. (2) It is, therefore, the third time.

### 3. Vocal Delivery: Volume
**Volume** refers to how loud or soft your voice is. 
* **Common Problems:** * *Monotone:* Never varying volume, causing listeners to lose interest.
    * *Too Soft:* Making it physically difficult for the audience to hear.
* **The Goal:** Vary your volume to maintain attention, but always speak loudly enough that the person furthest in the back of the room can hear you comfortably.

**Volume Scaling Exercise:**
Practice escalating your volume through these four levels:
* **Level 1:** I am speaking softly.
* **Level 2:** I am speaking at a normal volume.
* **Level 3:** I am speaking loudly.
* **Level 4:** I AM SPEAKING VERY LOUDLY.

**Application Exercise:**
* Sorry, I can't hear you.
* I'm sorry. I can't hear you.
* Can you speak up? I can't hear you.
* SPEAK UP! I CAN'T HEAR YOU.
* I want you to come home now.
* I want you to come home now.
* You need to come home now.
* You need to come home RIGHT NOW!

### 4. Vocal Delivery: Rate
**Rate** refers to how quickly or slowly you speak.
* **Common Problem:** Speaking so fast that listeners cannot process your words.
* **The Goal:** An ideal speaking rate is approximately **150 words per minute**. This is slow enough for listeners to understand, yet fast enough to keep their interest.

**Pacing Exercise (The 150-Word Test):**
Read the following 150-word paragraph aloud while timing yourself. 
* **Too Fast:** Less than 60 seconds (Slow down!).
* **Ideal Rate:** 60 to 75 seconds.
* **Too Slow:** Over 75 seconds (Speed up slightly!).

> "Your rate of speech will be perfect if it is slow enough for your listeners to understand yet fast enough to keep their interest. An ideal rate of speech is approximately one hundred fifty words per minute. There is an easy way to learn and practice how to speak at this rate. Choose a simple passage from a book, magazine, or newspaper that is one hundred fifty words. Count them to be sure. Read the passage while you time yourself in seconds. If the reading took you sixty to seventy-five seconds, your rate of speech is great. If you finished reading in less than a minute, slow down your rate of speech. Read the passage again. Strive to take at least one minute to finish reading. After several practice sessions, you will learn how it feels to speak at the ideal rate of one hundred fifty words per minute."
