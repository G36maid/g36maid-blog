## A

### 1
Let's consider a tree structure with two individuals, Person A and Person B, choosing different leaf nodes and moving towards diameter of the tree at a constant speed. We want to prove that they will inevitably meet or collide.

#### If A and B are on the same diameter:

In this case, as they are moving at the same speed, they will meet at the midpoint of the diameter. This is because they cover equal distances in equal times.
#### If A and B are on different diameters:

In this case, there must be a shortest path connecting A and B. One of the individuals will reach the midpoint of this path first. When this happens, the other person must be at some point on the path, and they will eventually meet or collide.
In summary, regardless of the specific leaf nodes chosen by A and B, they will either meet at the midpoint of a common diameter or on the shortest path connecting their chosen nodes. Therefore, it can be concluded that the two individuals will inevitably meet or collide.

### 2
**Algorithm for Verifying if a Node is on Any Diameter:**

1. Use the algorithm mentioned below (in problem A-3) to calculate the maximum diameter and its corresponding quantity for the entire tree.
2. While traversing each node, examine whether the diameter of the left subtree and the right subtree of that node includes the node itself.
3. If the sum of the distance to the deepest leaf node in the left subtree and the right subtree of a node is equal to the maximum diameter of the entire tree, and the diameter quantity of that node is not zero, then you can confirm that the node is on some diameter of the entire tree.

This verification approach relies on the known maximum diameter and diameter quantity, determining if each node is part of some diameter. The confirmation process can be integrated with the recursive traversal of the entire tree, maintaining a time complexity within the O(n) range.

### 3
**Algorithm Description:**

1. Recursively traverse each node of the tree.
2. For each node, calculate the distance to the deepest leaf node and the height of the subtree rooted at that node.
3. At each node, compute the diameter length as the sum of the distance to the deepest leaf node in the left subtree and the right subtree.
4. At each node, compute the diameter quantity as the product of the diameter quantities in the left and right subtrees.
5. During each diameter length and quantity calculation, update the global maximum diameter length and total quantity.

**Correctness Proof:**

1. According to the properties of trees, every diameter must pass through a certain node, ensuring that the algorithm recursively considers each node as the root.
2. Since the algorithm covers each node and calculates diameter length and quantity at each node, the final statistics of the maximum diameter length and total quantity cover the entire tree.

**Time Complexity Analysis:**

1. Each node is visited only once due to the recursive traversal.
2. Constant operations (calculating diameter length and quantity) are performed at each node.
3. Therefore, the overall time complexity of the algorithm is O(n), where n is the number of nodes in the tree.

### 1
**Algorithm Description:**

Given an N*M maze where the objective is to move from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner, we can employ a graph-based approach to represent the maze with multiple layers, each corresponding to a different direction. Nodes in the graph represent coordinates and facing direction. Edges between nodes are associated with movement costs (a for horizontal and b for vertical) and direction change costs. We utilize Dijkstra's algorithm to find the shortest path in this multi-layered graph.

1. **Graph Representation:**
   - Nodes: Each grid cell in the N*M maze corresponds to a node in the graph.
   - Edges: Each node can have up to 4 edges (representing movement in different directions).
   - Graph space complexity: O(N*M).

2. **Dijkstra's Algorithm:**
   - Use a priority queue to efficiently select the node with the minimum cost.
   - Inserting a node into the priority queue takes O(log N) time.
   - Extracting the minimum priority node takes O(log N) time.
   - The priority queue's size is at most N*M.
   - Dijkstra's algorithm time complexity: O((N*M) * log(N*M)).

**Overall Time Complexity:**
The overall time complexity is dominated by the Dijkstra's algorithm, making it O((N*M) * log(N*M)). This complexity is acceptable for practical purposes, but it's essential to ensure efficient implementation and optimization for real-world performance.
